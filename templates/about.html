WHY THIS PROJECT EXISTS

Why I Created S.C.A.M Don't CRAM

Many students know they need to study, but they are not always sure which methods are actually effective. SCAM Don't CRAM was created to help students explore their study habits and then connect those habits with practical, evidence-based strategies.

What does S.C.A.M stand for?

S

Study

Use effective strategies instead of relying only on last-minute revision.

C

Chill

Build healthier study habits and reduce unnecessary stress caused by cramming.

A

Ace

Use strong learning techniques to improve understanding, memory and preparation.

M

Master

Develop study habits you can keep using across subjects and future learning.

What the research found

A major finding from my research was that popular “learning styles” are often misunderstood. Research by Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork found no credible evidence that students learn better simply because teaching is matched to a preferred sensory style such as visual, auditory or kinesthetic.

Instead, stronger evidence supports strategies such as retrieval practice, spaced practice and interleaving. These techniques can feel harder than rereading or highlighting, but research suggests they support stronger long-term retention.

My research also found that factors such as persistence, responsibility and self-motivation are more closely linked with academic success than any single learning-style label.

So what does the quiz actually do?

The quiz explores your current study preferences and habits. It does not diagnose you as a fixed “type” of learner.

Your results are intended to suggest strategies you may want to try, while encouraging you to use evidence-based approaches such as self-testing, spaced revision and active practice.

Different subjects may also require different approaches. For example, diagrams may be useful for visual-spatial content such as geometry, while written explanation may be more suitable for language-based tasks.

Why trust this website?

The recommendations in SCAM Don't CRAM were informed by academic research rather than only personal opinion. The project also considered privacy, accessibility, usability and the ethical risk of giving students misleading study advice.

The goal is not to tell students that there is only one correct way for them to learn. Instead, the goal is to help students understand their habits, try effective strategies, and make better study decisions over time.

Research used in this project

  • Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008). Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 9(3), 105–119.
  • Riener, C., & Willingham, D. (2010). The Myth of Learning Styles. Change, 42(5), 32–35.
  • Snyder, R. F. (1999). The Relationship between Learning Styles/Multiple Intelligences and Academic Achievement of High School Students. The High School Journal, 83(2), 11–20.
  • Damberg, C. L. et al. (2011). An Evaluation of the Use of Performance Measures in Health Care. RAND Corporation.
